# Design Note — Guided Questions About AI

Origin: Aaron, ~14:00, after the team conversation. Locked in as the project at
14:05 (see [D-004](decisions.md)).

## The observation that started it

The July 21 room was full of **questions**, not just fear. Of ~44 cards
transcribed, roughly half are questions; ~14 are fears; ~8 are hopes. People
weren't mostly saying *"AI is bad."* They were saying *"I don't know how to think
about this."* That's confusion and risk-aversion, not opposition — and it's
addressable in an afternoon.

Aaron's original list of topics: what AI can and can't do · when to use it and
when not to · when it gets things wrong · preserving critical thinking · fostering
learning vs. creating reliance · how to use it · pitfalls.

## The trap we're deliberately avoiding

Two of the sharpest cards on the wall:

> "How can we make these tools resistive — to make us stronger rather than making
> us more comfortable."

> "Will youth be able to differentiate between facts and AI?"

An authoritative FAQ about AI — however well branched — is the thing those cards
warn about. It would be an AI telling people what to think about AI, demoed to the
people who wrote the warning. It's also the most-written content on the internet:
a generic "what can AI do" tree has nothing to do with Alaska, and the brief said
*for Alaskans* twice.

**So: the product does not hand out verdicts.** Its output is the tradeoffs named,
questions to ask yourself, and an explicit statement of where it might be wrong.
It *models* critical thinking rather than lecturing about it. That is simultaneously
the answer to Aaron's "how do we preserve critical thinking" bullet and the defense
against the room's strongest objection.

## The asset nobody else has

~50 real questions, in real handwriting, from real Anchorage people — some of whom
will be in the room at 6:00 PM tonight. Already transcribed in
[../00-context/prior-session-2026-07-21-cards.md](../00-context/prior-session-2026-07-21-cards.md).

Using the actual cards as entry points is what makes this local rather than
generic, and it is emotionally strong in the demo: a guest sees a neighbor's
question — possibly their own — and watches it get taken seriously.

## Shape

**Hybrid, not a pure decision tree.** A static tree is brittle and reads as dated.
A blank chat box is exactly what intimidates a risk-averse user. So:

1. **Guided entry** — pick a real card from the wall, or describe your own situation
2. **2–3 fixed orienting questions** — establish who you are and what's actually at stake
3. **Generative body** — reasoning specific to that situation
4. **Structured output**, always the same three parts:
   - the tradeoffs, named honestly
   - three questions to ask yourself
   - where this tool might be wrong

Part 4 is not decoration. It is the product's whole thesis.

## Scope — three topics, not nine

Nine shallow branches demo worse than three deep ones. Ranked by card-wall
frequency:

| # | Topic | Why it made the cut |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Kids and parenting** | Most frequent theme; most concrete card on the wall (9/12/15-year-olds); the 6:00 guest audience is parents |
| 2 | **Reliance vs. cognitive development** | The room's own strongest caution ("the less a person has to learn, the more they give up") |
| 3 | **Where it gets things wrong / when to trust it** | The practical one; answers the "differentiate facts from AI" card |

Aaron's other six topics become the **"what's next"** slide — which makes the demo
read as intentional rather than unfinished.

## Known risks

- **Tone.** This slides into preachy very easily. Mitigation: test with a real
  parent or teacher in the room by ~4:30. There are plenty of both.
- **Nine-topic creep.** If branches start multiplying after 3:00, that's the scope
  cut arriving — refuse it.
- **The irony check.** Any time the output starts sounding like a verdict, it has
  drifted. Re-read this file.

## Open design questions

- Does the user *see* the handwritten card, or just the transcribed text?
  **Assumed yes — show the real card image**, since the handwriting is the proof
  it's a neighbor and not stock content. Overturn if it hurts readability.
- Do we credit / anonymize card writers? Default: anonymous, no names shown.
